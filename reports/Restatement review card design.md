# Put the arithmetic before the story

The reviewer should have at their fingertips the **arithmetic and the accounting footprint of the change**: old value, new value, the delta, which other lines moved with it, and which candidate causes that pattern rules in or out. The LLM's quote-backed narrative should come after that, as a hypothesis to test rather than a verdict to approve. The independent evidence is consistent on this. **Explanations raise acceptance of AI answers whether or not the AI is right** ([Bansal et al., CHI 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2006.14779)). In a meta-analysis of 106 experiments, **neither explanations nor confidence displays significantly improved human+AI performance** ([Vaccaro et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)). The one reliable lever is **making verification cheaper than re-deriving the answer** ([Vasconcelos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). The GE case fits this pattern exactly. A real quote about the GE HealthCare spin-off made a wrong cause look verified. A one-line footprint check would have caught it: "total net income moved, and a spin-off reclassified to discontinued operations cannot move total net income." The way to remove drudgery without creating a button-clicker is to automate the hunting (finding filings, diffing, locating the note, tying numbers, running footprint checks) and leave the human the judgment calls the evidence says only they make well. Their decision should be recorded as a structured correction with a reason code, not an approve bit. That makes each decision a labeled failure in a LangSmith dataset, measured against a random audit sample of auto-passed items so the error rate can be estimated honestly. One caution about the evidence: almost everything known about finance-tool review UIs comes from vendors, and **no independent source measures how analysts actually re-spread restated figures or how long it takes**.

## The GE failure is the textbook "convincingly wrong" case, and citations alone do not fix it

The failure the agent hit has a name in the literature. Si et al. found that LLM explanations made fact-checkers faster than search engines at similar accuracy, but readers **over-relied when the explanation was wrong**. Adding the LLM explanation on top of retrieved passages gave **no complementary benefit over the passages alone** ([Si et al., NAACL 2024](https://aclanthology.org/2024.naacl-long.81)). Citation-style interfaces have their own problem. An audit of four generative search engines found only **51.5% of generated sentences fully supported** by their citations and only **74.5% of citations actually supporting their sentence**, which the authors called a "facade of trustworthiness" ([Liu et al., EMNLP Findings 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2304.09848v2)). The GE explanation passes the check the pipeline already runs (the quote exists verbatim) and fails the one that matters (the quote states the cause of *this* change to *this* line item in *this* period). A verified quote proves the quote is real. It does not prove the cause.

The human-factors literature says the reviewer will not reliably catch this without help. Automation bias shows up in experts and in teams, and it **"cannot be prevented by training or instructions"** ([Parasuraman & Manzey 2010](https://journals.sagepub.com/doi/10.1177/0018720810376055)). In healthcare decision support, erroneous advice was **26% more likely to be followed** when the system was present, and **6–11% of initially correct decisions were switched to wrong ones** after the reviewer saw the advice ([Goddard et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751)). Approving or rejecting a cause is a "decision task", the category where the Vaccaro meta-analysis found human+AI teams **underperform the better of human or AI alone (g = −0.27)** ([Vaccaro et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)). A card that leads with fluent prose and a green "quote verified" badge is set up to produce rubber-stamped errors.

Vasconcelos et al. reframe the problem in a way that makes design possible. People decide whether to check an AI explanation by weighing cost against benefit. Explanations reduced overreliance **only when they were easier to verify than the task itself** and when being right mattered ([Vasconcelos et al. 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2212.06823)). The design goal is therefore narrower than "explain better". It is to make the decisive check (do the numbers and the cause's footprint agree?) a one-glance operation, so checking is the cheapest thing the reviewer can do.

## Accounting footprints turn a judgment call into a one-glance check

Each restatement cause leaves a predictable footprint in the statements, set by ASC 250, ASC 260 and the discontinued-operations rules. Splits are applied retroactively and change **only share counts and per-share data** ([Deloitte DART EPS Roadmap](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc260-10/roadmap-earnings-per-share/chapter-8-other-considerations?combine)). A retrospective change to income requires prior EPS to be restated "as if" the adjusted income had been originally reported ([Deloitte DART](https://dart.deloitte.com/USDART/home/codification/presentation/asc260-10/roadmap-earnings-per-share/chapter-8-other-considerations?combine)). Error corrections must disclose the effect on each line item and per-share amount, the cumulative effect on opening retained earnings, and the tax effects ([PwC Viewpoint 30.7](https://viewpoint.pwc.com/dt/us/en/pwc/accounting_guides/financial_statement_/financial_statement___18_US/chapter_30_accountin_US/307_correction_of_an_US.html)). ASU 2018-12 (LDTI) took effect for non-SRC SEC filers in **fiscal years beginning after December 15, 2022**, and adopters restated comparatives, generally from a **January 1, 2021 transition date** ([Cherry Bekaert](https://www.cbh.com/insights/articles/what-ldti-means-for-insurance-accounting/)). That is the window in which GE's FY2022 figure would be recast in a later 10-K.

| Cause | Can move | Should not move | Card check this enables |
|---|---|---|---|
| Stock split | Shares, EPS, per-share data | Dollar totals | Whole-number ratio on every per-share line; dollars unchanged |
| Discontinued ops / spin-off | Continuing-ops lines, offset in disc-ops income | Total net income, total equity | Does continuing + discontinued sum to the old total? |
| Standard adoption (e.g. ASU 2018-12) | Prior line items, opening retained earnings (and AOCI for LDTI) | Cash | Is the period inside the standard's transition window? |
| Error correction | Any line | — | Does it tie to an "as previously reported / adjustment / as revised" table? |

This table is the project's inference from the cited standards. The research notes flag that the claim "a disc-ops reclassification leaves total net income unchanged" was **not sourced to ASC 205-20 text** in this research pass, although it is standard practice. Confirm it against KPMG or Deloitte guidance before encoding it as a hard rule. With that caveat, the table is the strongest single idea for the card because it moves the GE catch from reviewer vigilance to deterministic code. The agent claimed a spin-off, net income moved from 225 to 339, and the footprint says a spin-off cannot do that. The card can then say "spin-off ruled out by footprint; standard adoption consistent (FY2022 inside the ASU 2018-12 window)" before the reviewer reads a word of prose.

Footprints also handle the most dangerous category, the error correction that does not call itself one. **Little-r revisions rose to about 76% of corrections by 2020, from about 35% in 2005** ([Cooley PubCo](https://cooleypubco.com/?p=23453)). The share of corrections using the word "restate" fell from **over 93% in 2006 to 44% in 2012** ([Audit Analytics](https://blog.auditanalytics.com/restatements-whats-in-a-name/)). Thompson's research found **45% of revisions met at least one SEC materiality criterion** ([Bloomberg Law](https://news.bloomberglaw.com/financial-accounting/companies-fixing-more-errors-by-revisions-than-restatements)), and the SEC's acting chief accountant warned in 2022 that materiality analyses "appear to be biased toward supporting an outcome that an error is not material" ([Cooley PubCo](https://cooleypubco.com/?p=23453)). A change that fits no benign footprint, has no 8-K Item 4.02, and appears near soft wording like "revised" or "immaterial error" is where a human reviewer adds the most value. The card should rank it highest, not treat it as one more case.

## What goes on the card, in what order, and what stays off it

The market sets the minimum. Click-to-source with the passage highlighted is standard across AlphaSense, Hebbia, Daloopa and Bloomberg ([AlphaSense help](https://help.alpha-sense.com/hc/en-us/articles/41666587181203-Interacting-with-Generative-Search); [Bloomberg](https://www.bloomberg.com/company/press/bloomberg-launches-ai-powered-earnings-call-summaries); [Daloopa docs](https://docs.daloopa.com/docs/one-click-audit)). Daloopa keeps an "original" and a "restated" record per data point instead of overwriting ([Daloopa docs](https://docs.daloopa.com/docs/fundamental-uniqueness-restatements)). MindBridge shows which named control points fired on a flagged transaction rather than a single score ([MindBridge whitepaper](https://info.mindbridge.ai/hubfs/docs-website/Whitepaper/MindBridgeAnalytics-AI-Audit-Methodology-Whitepaper.pdf)). All of these are **vendor descriptions, with no independent usability evidence behind them**, so treat them as conventions, not proof. The research found **no product that shows competing causes or "why not X" reasoning**. That gap reflects a limited search, but it means the candidate-cause panel below has no tested precedent in finance.

Combining those conventions with the independent evidence gives a proposed order that puts verifiable facts first and the AI's story last:

| Order | Element | Why it is there | Evidence basis |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Old value, new value and delta, each deep-linked to a highlighted cell with filing date and accession number | The thing being judged, verifiable in one click | Vendor convention (Daloopa, DataSnipper); auditability norm |
| 2 | Co-movement: what else moved (total NI, EPS, continuing ops, disc-ops line, retained earnings) | Supplies the footprint | Inference from ASC 250/260 |
| 3 | Named checks, passed and failed (footprint, standard window, 8-K 4.02 present, tie-out to reconciliation table, quote states cause) | A failed rule tells the reviewer where to look; a probability does not | MindBridge/Instabase pattern (vendor); Vasconcelos |
| 4 | Candidate causes, each marked consistent or ruled out, with the evidence for each | Contrastive display reduces overreliance on convincingly wrong explanations | Si et al. (independent, N = 80) |
| 5 | The quote, with the span that states the cause highlighted, and a flag when it names the event but not the effect on this line | Targets the GE failure directly | Liu et al.; Vasconcelos |
| 6 | The agent's explanation, short and collapsed, with specific first-person hedges ("the passage does not tie the spin-off to net income") | Kept, but made less prominent | Kim et al. FAccT 2024 (N = 404); Goddard on advice prominence |
| 7 | Decision: confirm cause / change cause (pick from taxonomy) / insufficient evidence, with a required reason code on non-confirm | Produces training signal, not just a click | Feedback-loop practice (below) |

Three things stay off the card or are hidden by default. The first is a **numeric LLM confidence score**. The best aggregate evidence shows confidence displays have no significant effect ([Vaccaro et al. 2024](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC11659167/)). An older review is more favorable ([Goddard et al. 2012](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3240751)), and the research surfaced no evidence that LLM-derived confidence in these tools is calibrated. Verbal hedges tied to a named weakness have better support ([Kim et al. 2024](https://www.arxiv.org/abs/2405.00623)). The second is the **full reasoning chain**. Hebbia exposes one per cell ([vantaige.io](https://vantaige.io/fr/ai-tool/hebbia), vendor via aggregator), but more fluent prose is exactly what raises acceptance regardless of correctness, so it belongs one click down. The third is **an equal-weight presentation of every item**. Clinical drug alerts are overridden in **49–96% of cases** ([van der Sijs et al. 2006](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC1447540)), while a hospital that tiered alerts by severity saw **100% acceptance of its most severe alerts versus 34%** at a hospital that did not ([Paterno et al. 2009](https://academic.oup.com/jamia/article/16/1/40/865495), an observational two-site comparison). Severity belongs at the top of the card and in how the queue is sorted.

The open design question is how much to hold back the AI's answer. There are three options, each with a cost.

**AI-first** (the current default) is fastest. It is also the condition where anchoring is strongest.

**Evidence-first** shows rows 1–5 and keeps the agent's narrative collapsed until the reviewer expands it or decides. It costs little, follows Goddard's finding that less prominent advice reduces bias, and suits the default card.

**Predict-first** asks the reviewer to pick a cause before seeing the agent's. It reduced agreement with wrong AI answers in a study of veterinary radiologists, and **did not lengthen task time** ([Fogliato et al., FAccT 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.09696)). That study had n = 19. The same design also reduced agreement with *correct* AI answers, and forcing designs got the **lowest user ratings** even though they cut overreliance most ([Buçinca et al., CSCW 2021](https://www.eecs.harvard.edu/~kgajos/papers/2021/bucinca2021trust.shtml)).

A reasonable compromise is evidence-first everywhere and predict-first only on the highest tier or a random sample. That fits the author's "not a button-clicker" goal: predict-first treats the reviewer as an analyst, but applied everywhere it would make them dislike the tool.

## Automate the hunting, keep the judgment, and watch the prevalence trap

Analysts and auditors do two kinds of work, and they divide cleanly. The research notes infer, from the structure of the disclosures (**no time study exists**), that the drudgery is mechanical: finding which filing first showed the new value, diffing every line, finding the explaining note behind soft wording, tying the change to the reconciliation table, and confirming that totals and EPS moved consistently. A single new 10-K silently restates every comparative number in it ([ARCP 10-K example](https://www.sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/0001507385/000150738515000030/arcp1231201410-k.htm)), and **none of the vendor docs reviewed expose the cause of a change as a structured field** ([Daloopa docs](https://docs.daloopa.com/docs/fundamental-uniqueness-restatements); [XBRL US guidance](https://xbrl.us/guidance/reporting-restated-values-in-the-face-of-the-financials/)). That is the gap filings-agent fills. Everything in rows 1–5 of the card should be produced by code or by tightly checked LLM extraction. That includes grouping related line items that share one root cause into a single card, which is plausible but has **no controlled evidence** behind it.

The human keeps the calls that need judgment: which cause applies when the checks conflict or the footprints overlap, whether a little-r revision looks like a disguised Big R, whether a recast series is still comparable, and whether the change matters to a thesis. These are also the calls where reviewer input most improves the agent, because a footprint rule cannot settle them.

Routing has a known trap. When errors are rare in what reaches a person, they miss more of them: in Wolfe's studies the miss rate was **0.30 at 1% target prevalence versus 0.07 at 50%**, and the effect is "easy to produce and surprisingly hard to cure" ([PMC3069706](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC3069706)). The current triage auto-passes clean splits and sends everything else to review, so the queue is error-dense and in no danger yet. The risk arrives as more causes get auto-pass rules, because the queue then fills with mostly-correct items that train the reviewer to approve. The evidence-backed defense is **seeded catch trials**: known-wrong explanations, with the GE spin-off claim as the first, mixed into the queue so prevalence stays up and each reviewer's catch rate becomes measurable. Mozannar et al. found that giving people well-chosen examples of when the AI is wrong improved how well they judged when to rely on it ([MIT VIS](https://vis.mit.edu/pubs/teaching-defer-exemplars.pdf)). Accountability helps independently: reviewers who saw themselves as accountable verified more and made fewer automation errors ([Automation bias summary of Skitka/Mosier](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Automation_bias)), so a short required rationale on high-tier approvals is cheap insurance. If the product ever serves EU high-risk uses, Article 14(4)(b) of the AI Act requires overseers to "remain aware of" automation bias ([Art. 14 text](https://cdn.presencis.com/regulations/eu-ai-act/article-14/)), and catch-trial override rates would be direct evidence that the system meets it.

## Each decision should become a labeled failure, measured against a random audit

The plumbing already exists. LangGraph's `interrupt()` and `Command(resume=...)` support approve, edit and validate patterns. Code before the interrupt re-runs on resume, so side effects such as writing feedback belong after it ([LangGraph interrupts](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langgraph/interrupts)). The standard `HumanInTheLoopMiddleware` payload carries only an action, its args and a `description`, with **no field for evidence or rationale**. Its `reject` message goes back to the model rather than to a durable store ([LangChain HITL docs](https://docs.langchain.com/oss/python/langchain/human-in-the-loop)). Because filings-agent calls `interrupt()` directly, the card's evidence should travel in a custom interrupt value, and the decision should be written separately to LangSmith. `create_feedback` accepts a score, a categorical value, a comment and a **`correction` field defined as "the proper ground truth for this run"**, and it can attach to the specific child run that failed, such as the cause-attribution step rather than extraction ([LangSmith SDK](https://langsmith-sdk.readthedocs.io/en/latest/client/langsmith.client.Client.create_feedback.html); [LangSmith feedback docs](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/attach-user-feedback)). Automation rules can route items on feedback to datasets or annotation queues, with a **0–100% sampling rate** and backfill ([LangSmith rules](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/rules)).

The mapping follows from that. A confirm writes `review_decision=1`. A changed cause writes `0`, with the corrected cause in `correction` and a reason code in `value`. The reason codes come from the failure taxonomy: wrong cause with a real quote, footprint violation, quote names the event but not the effect, insufficient evidence, extraction error. The reason code matters more than the bit. Practitioner guidance treats free-text critiques, clustered into a failure taxonomy, as "the most important activity in evals", and recommends binary labels over Likert scales ([Husain & Shankar, Evals FAQ](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/evals-faq/)). Anthropic's agent-eval guidance says **20–50 tasks drawn from real failures** is a good start. It also says regression suites should stay near 100% pass, while capability evals start low and "graduate" ([Anthropic Engineering](https://www.anthropic.com/engineering/demystifying-evals-for-ai-agents)). The existing "corrections become regression tests" step fits that model. Corrections the agent still fails should first go into a capability set and graduate to regression once they pass.

Four measurement disciplines keep this loop honest. First, **selective labels**: if humans label only what triage flags, the labels are not a random sample, and the error rate of auto-passed items cannot be estimated from them ([Lakkaraju et al., KDD 2017](https://www.cs.cornell.edu/home/kleinber/kdd17-selective.pdf)). A second automation rule should send a small random fraction of auto-passed splits to an audit queue, tagged so they never mix with flagged items. Bounding an error rate near 1% takes hundreds of audits (standard binomial statistics; **no source gives a recommended audit percentage**). Second, measure reviewers with an **appropriateness-of-reliance split**: correct overrides on seeded-wrong items, and wrong rejections of correct items, tracked separately, because approval rate alone hides rubber-stamping ([Schemmer et al., IUI 2023](https://arxiv.org/abs/2302.02187v3)). Third, if an LLM judge later grades explanations, calibrate it with LangSmith's Align Evaluator, but report **TPR/TNR rather than its percent-agreement "alignment score"**, which looks high on imbalanced data where most items pass ([LangSmith docs](https://docs.langchain.com/langsmith/improve-judge-evaluator-feedback); [Husain](https://hamel.dev/blog/posts/llm-judge/)). Fourth, **version the rubric**. Shankar et al. found that "grading outputs helps users define criteria", so labels made under an early taxonomy may not be comparable with later ones ([Shankar et al. 2024](https://arxiv.org/abs/2404.12272)). With one reviewer, the "benevolent dictator" model avoids an agreement problem. With several, route disagreements to adjudication before they reach the regression set. The research found **no documented LangSmith inter-annotator agreement report and no built-in gold-question seeding**, so both need custom code.

## Conclusion

The research changes what "remove the drudgery" means. The drudgery is not clicking approve. It is the reconciliation work a reviewer would otherwise have to do to check the AI honestly. Once code does that work, the reviewer's job shrinks to the part the evidence says humans actually add: deciding between causes the footprint leaves open, and spotting the little-r revision that ought to have been a Big R. The design that respects the reviewer as an analyst therefore also hands the most useful training signal back to the agent, because every non-confirm arrives with a typed reason on exactly the cases code could not settle. The GE case shows that the most valuable automation is deterministic falsification of the agent's cause, not a better explanation of it.

The gaps are real, and they should set the next experiments rather than be designed around. There is **no independent evidence on analyst re-spreading workflow or its time cost**, **no independent usability study of any finance vendor's review UI**, **no tested precedent for showing competing causes in finance**, and **no controlled study of evidence-first versus AI-first review for causal text explanations**. The cheapest way to fill them is to instrument filings-agent itself. Log dwell time and decision per card, seed the GE claim and a few synthetic footprint violations as catch trials, and run evidence-first against predict-first on a sample. Within weeks that would give the project its own small-n but domain-specific answer, which nobody else has published.
